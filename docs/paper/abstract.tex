Machine-learning surrogates for computational fluid dynamics predict steady flow fields
far faster than classical solvers, and the standard proposal for trusting them is to have
the surrogate check itself against the governing equations. We show that this check is
built on an operator the truth itself cannot pass. On $200/200$ real AirfRANS cases the
monitored discrete Reynolds-averaged residual of the \emph{ground-truth} field is
substantially nonzero (mean $0.192$), while the physically wrong uniform-freestream field
scores an exact zero---so the objective prefers a wrong field to the truth. This floor is
not a resolution artifact. The monitored operator is an inconsistent discretisation, so
nothing forces its floor to vanish; measured over $24$ cases at
$128^2$--$512^2$ it does not merely persist but \emph{grows}, rising in $21/24$ cases,
and neither restoring the omitted closure term nor using a higher-order interpolant
removes it. Descending the residual is therefore harmful in a specific, measurable way:
started at the exact ground truth, with no network involved, gradient descent cuts the
residual by $84\%$ while driving field error from zero to the range a trained surrogate
starts in, and the residual-selected iterate is strictly worse than the error-optimal one
in $24/24$ cases. The boundary is the operator, not the problem class---residual
correction succeeds where the residual is the solver's own. What survives is narrower and
we quantify it: the residual ranks errors usefully but is matched by a physics-free
uncertainty score except on drag, and a conformal layer retains calibrated coverage.
