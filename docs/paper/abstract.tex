Machine-learning CFD surrogates are far faster than solvers, but their gains are reported
only against other learned models. Kernel interpolation over seven scalars from the
AirfRANS case name---no network, no flow-field learning---gives $8.4\times$ lower
volume-pressure error than a matched-budget Transolver on an area-uniform $128^2$ raster,
and \textbf{no advantage on any channel} under the dataset's own node measure, which
places $64\%$ of its mesh inside $0.05$ chord of the wall against $1.2\%$ of the raster's
area. Re-weighting the identical predictions takes the pressure ratio to
$\mathbf{0.44\times}$ and the streamwise-velocity ratio from $6.2\times$ against the
interpolator to $86\times$; the ranking is a property of the weighting, which no published
AirfRANS comparison states. The raster's own round-trip error at those nodes exceeds the
surrogate's by $413\times$: the protocol cannot adjudicate near-wall skill at all. What it
does resolve is where each method's error lives: outward from the wall the
interpolation-to-Transolver error ratio on $u$ falls monotonically from $906\times$ to
$0.31\times$, while $71.9\%$ of Transolver's own error lies beyond $0.5$ chord. A surrogate
buys the first cell and the closure. The benchmark's primary metric cannot see that: a
train-fit regression on the case name ranks official lift at $0.9821$ and drag at
$0.9318$, above all four published lift entries---decisively for only two---and above every
drag entry, three of four intervals spanning zero. Nor does the \texttt{reynolds} split
test Reynolds generalisation: no test case has an in-range inlet velocity, yet a
nondimensional interpolator degrades $3\%$ where a dimensional one degrades
$5.2$--$10.1\times$.
