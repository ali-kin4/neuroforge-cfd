Machine-learning CFD surrogates predict steady flow fields far faster than solvers, but
their reported gains are measured only against other learned models. Kernel
interpolation over seven scalars parsed from the AirfRANS case name---with no network
and no flow-field learning---scored through the identical protocol and split, beats a
Transolver trained on the same data on volume pressure ($8.4\times$), cross-flow velocity
($2.6\times$) and both force errors ($C_l$ $1.18\%$ against $5.81\%$; $C_d$ $2.39\%$
against $8.99\%$). It loses on streamwise velocity ($6.5\times$) and eddy viscosity
($15\times$), and so loses $6.4\times$ on the four-channel standardised objective the
surrogate trains on. The boundary is sharp: $92\%$ of the interpolator's $u$ error and
$90\%$ of its $v$ error lie within $0.02c$ of the wall, $0.5\%$ of cells, and beyond
$0.05c$ it reproduces every channel at $R^2\ge0.9996$. What a surrogate buys here is
the first cell off the wall and the closure.
The benchmark's declared primary metric cannot see that difference. A regression on the
case name, fit on train and scored on test, ranks official lift at $0.9821$
$[0.9737,0.9866]$ and drag at $0.9318$ $[0.8981,0.9531]$; every baseline in the
AirfRANS paper falls below both. Its \texttt{reynolds} split does not test Reynolds
generalisation either: no test case has an in-range inlet velocity, yet a
nondimensional interpolator degrades $3\%$ where a dimensional one degrades
$5.2$--$10.1\times$.
We also audit the surrogate-side physics residual: it ranks errors and certifies them,
but the ground truth itself fails the check, so it cannot be descended.
