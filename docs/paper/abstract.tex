Machine-learning CFD surrogates predict steady flow fields far faster than solvers, but
their gains are reported only against other learned models. Kernel interpolation over
seven scalars parsed from the AirfRANS case name---no network, no flow-field
learning---scored through the identical protocol, gives \textbf{$88\%$ lower}
volume-pressure error than a Transolver trained on the same data ($8.4\times$), $62\%$
lower cross-flow error, and force errors \textbf{$4.9\times$ lower on lift} and
$3.8\times$ lower on drag. It loses on streamwise velocity ($6.5\times$) and eddy
viscosity ($15\times$), hence $6.4\times$ on the four-channel standardised training
objective. The boundary is sharp: $92\%$ of its $u$ error and $90\%$ of its $v$ error lie
inside $0.02$ chord of the wall, \textbf{$0.5\%$ of cells}, while \textbf{$98.7\%$ of the
domain}, beyond $0.05$ chord, is reproduced at $R^2\ge0.9996$. A surrogate buys the first
cell off the wall and the closure---where the benchmark's own authors located their
models' failure. The benchmark's declared primary metric cannot see that difference: a
train-fit regression on the case name ranks official lift at
$0.9821$ $[0.9737,0.9866]$ and drag at $0.9318$ $[0.8981,0.9531]$, and \textbf{four of
five} published baselines fall below it on lift while three of four drag intervals span
zero. The \texttt{reynolds} split does not test Reynolds generalisation either---no test
case has an in-range inlet velocity, yet a nondimensional interpolator degrades $3\%$
where a dimensional one degrades $5.2$--$10.1\times$. The surrogate-side physics
residual, audited here, ranks errors and certifies them but cannot be descended: the
ground truth itself fails the check.
